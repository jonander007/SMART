AHP Expert Aggregator — Statistical Methodology

What does this tool do?

Aggregates pairwise comparison judgments from multiple AHP experts into a single consensus result. It detects statistically anomalous values (outliers), excludes them, and recomputes a clean aggregated mean — producing a defensible group judgment ready to load into SMART.

Input format

Each .smt file is a JSON exported from the SMART questionnaire via Download JSON (.smt). It contains a Criteria Comparison section (criteria and subcriteria pairwise judgments) and a Rating Scales Comparison section (pairwise judgments between qualitative rating-scale options), both on Saaty's ratio scale (1/9 to 9). Both sections are aggregated independently and shown as separate groups in the results tables below. Load one file per expert — no limit on the number of experts.

The Rating Table section (the alternatives' actual scores) is not aggregated — those are objective measurements, not subjective pairwise judgments, and are taken unchanged from the first loaded file.

Key design principle — log scale

The Saaty scale is a ratio scale. The gap between 1 and 3 represents the same "intensity of preference" as the gap between 1/3 and 1, or between 3 and 9. A linear Z-score would unfairly penalise small values (e.g. 1/9) relative to large ones (e.g. 9). All statistics in this tool — mean, standard deviation, and Z-score — are therefore computed in log space, making the scale symmetric and uniform.

log(1/9) and log(9) are equidistant from log(1) → same Z-score

AIJ — Aggregation of Individual Judgments

Recommended when experts act as a group (e.g. workshop). For each pair (i,j) the group judgment is the geometric mean of all m individual judgments:

a_ij(group) = ( ∏ a_ij(k) )^(1/m) for k = 1…m experts

Equivalent to arithmetic mean in log space: mean(log(a_ij)) then exponentiated back. Preserves reciprocity (a_ij = 1/a_ji) and is consistent with Saaty's ratio scale.

AIP — Aggregation of Individual Priorities

Used when experts respond independently. The group judgment is the arithmetic mean of individual values:

a_ij(group) = (1/m) · Σ a_ij(k) for k = 1…m experts

More intuitive to communicate, but does not preserve reciprocity and is less consistent with the ratio scale.

When to use each

AspectAIJ (geometric mean)AIP (arithmetic mean)

Outlier sensitivityHighModerate
AIJ and AIP are not mathematically equivalent and may produce different rankings when expert disagreement is high. Always document which method was used and justify the choice.

Z-score in log space

Because the Saaty scale is a ratio scale, all outlier detection is performed in logarithmic space. For each criterion pair, the log-mean and log-standard-deviation are computed across all experts, and the Z-score for expert k is:

Z_k = | log(x_k) − log_mean | / log_σ

where log_mean = mean of log(values) and log_σ = sample std dev of log(values). Any value with Z > threshold is flagged and excluded from the clean mean.

Why log space matters

In linear space, a value of 1/9 ≈ 0.11 sits very close to the mean when most experts give values around 1–3. Its deviation looks tiny numerically even though it represents the strongest possible disagreement. In log space:

|log(1/9) − log(1)| = |log(9) − log(1)| = log(9) ≈ 2.20

The scale is now uniform — every unit step in log space represents the same ratio change.

Sample standard deviation

Uses Bessel's correction (n−1 denominator) for an unbiased estimate:

log_σ = sqrt( Σ(log(x_k) − log_mean)² / (m−1) )

With fewer than 2 experts σ = 0 and no outlier detection is performed.

MAD — Median Absolute Deviation (recommended)

The classic Z-score has a known weakness with small panels (n ≤ 6): a single outlier inflates σ, making its own Z-score appear moderate — the masking effect. The MAD-based score (Iglewicz & Hoaglin, 1993) is immune to this because it uses the median instead of the mean:

MAD_score = 0.6745 × | log(x_k) − median(log) | / MAD

where MAD = median( |log(x_i) − median(log)| ). The constant 0.6745 scales the score to match a standard Z-score for normally distributed data, so the same thresholds apply. When MAD = 0 (all values identical), the mean absolute deviation is used as fallback.

The classic Z-score is provided for reference. For small expert panels, MAD is strongly recommended as it correctly flags the lone-dissenter pattern (e.g. one expert gives 3, four give 0.5).

Coefficient of Variation (CV%)

The Divergence tab ranks all pairs by their CV% — the ratio of standard deviation to mean, expressed as a percentage. CV% is computed in linear space to remain interpretable as "percentage of the mean":

CV% = (σ_linear / x̄_linear) × 100

A high CV% indicates significant disagreement among experts on that pair regardless of the absolute values involved.

CV% rangeLevelInterpretation
< 20%LowStrong consensus
20–50%ModerateNotable disagreement — worth discussing
> 50%HighSignificant conflict — review with panel

Exported .smt

The aggregated .smt substitutes each pairwise value — in both Criteria Comparison and Rating Scales Comparison — with the clean mean (after outlier exclusion). The Analyst field records the method, threshold, and number of experts. Open directly in SMART via File → Open to compute the final alternative ranking.

Always verify Consistency Ratio (CR < 0.10) in SMART after loading the aggregated file.
